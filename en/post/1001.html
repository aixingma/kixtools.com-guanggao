M3U8 Explained: Inside the Playlist File That Powers HLS Streaming

A Playlist Is Not a Video

When someone shares a .m3u8 link, they are not handing you the movie itself. What arrives is a small UTF-8 text file — an index, really — telling your player where every piece of the actual footage lives on disk or on a CDN. Picture a table of contents whose chapters happen to be a few seconds of H.264 video each, wrapped in TS containers.

This split is deliberate. Keeping the index tiny while chopping the media into short chunks lets ordinary HTTP infrastructure do the heavy lifting: browsers already speak HTTP fluently, edge nodes cache it well, and no exotic socket has to stay open between client and server.

Decoding the Tags Line by Line

Here is a trimmed-down media playlist of the kind that floats around the public internet:

#EXTM3U
#EXT-X-VERSION:6
#EXT-X-TARGETDURATION:6
#EXT-X-MEDIA-SEQUENCE:0
#EXTINF:5.76,
chunk-0001.ts
#EXTINF:5.80,
chunk-0002.ts
#EXT-X-ENDLIST

Every meaningful line starts with a hash, and each tag answers a specific question:

  • #EXTM3U — the signature. No player will even attempt to parse a file without it.
  • #EXT-X-TARGETDURATION — an upper bound, in seconds, that no single chunk should exceed. Values between 4 and 10 are typical.
  • #EXTINF — the exact runtime of the chunk listed directly beneath it. Players add these up to draw the progress bar.
  • #EXT-X-ENDLIST — the closing stamp. Present means the recording is finished; absent means the camera is still rolling.

Master Playlists and Variant Streams

Open a production link and you will often find the first file you fetch lists no video at all — only more playlists. That is the master playlist, HLS's version of a channel selector:

#EXTM3U
#EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=2149000,RESOLUTION=1280x720
720p/index.m3u8
#EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=738000,RESOLUTION=640x360
360p/index.m3u8

Each entry describes one rendition, encoded at a different resolution and bitrate. The player measures available throughput, samples the best match, and quietly steps up or down as conditions change. That mechanism is adaptive bitrate streaming — the reason a clip softens slightly on hotel Wi-Fi instead of freezing mid-scene.

Live Windows vs On-Demand Files

One format, two very different delivery models. For on-demand content, every chunk was produced in advance and #EXT-X-ENDLIST sits at the bottom — the playlist is a complete menu you can seek through freely.

Live broadcasts behave differently. The encoder keeps appending fresh entries while dropping old ones from the top, so the file behaves like a window sliding across the broadcast. Seek backwards past the window's left edge and the footage is simply gone. This is also why capturing a live event means following the playlist as it mutates rather than fetching it once.

See One for Yourself

Nothing cements the concept like watching a real playlist scroll past. Grab any stream you are allowed to use, paste it into the online player, and open your browser's developer tools to watch chunks arrive in order.
